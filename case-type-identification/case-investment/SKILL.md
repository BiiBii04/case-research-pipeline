# Finance / Investment Case — Checklist and Framework

This skill turns a case-solving method into something usable live, during a competition, under
time pressure. It is one member of a family of case-type skills (`case-brand-communication`,
`case-profit-optimization`, `case-supply-chain`, `case-ma`, `case-product-launch`, `case-investment`, and later
`case-market-entry`, `case-growth-strategy`, `case-turnaround`, `case-pricing`) that together cover the case
types a team might draw. Read the "How this fits with other case-type skills" section below first if more than
one case-type skill is loaded — it explains how to keep the final answer coherent instead of stapling together
separate mini-answers.

The content here is an original, rewritten summary of standard financial-statement analysis and capital-
budgeting methods (P&L structure and ratio analysis, payback period, perpetuity-style investment valuation,
return on invested capital). Keep this skill team-internal; don't redistribute outside the competition team.

**By design, this skill is company-agnostic.** It knows how to recognise a finance/investment case and how to
solve one — it does not know anything about any specific company, real or fictional, and doesn't reference any
particular team's research files by name. That's deliberate: the checklist and framework need to work unchanged
on whatever brief actually drops, for this competition or a future one, without being tuned (or biased) toward
one team's prep material. If your team has built company-specific research for the current competition, keep the
pointer to it in a separate, clearly-labelled file outside this skill (see Section 5) — don't fold it into this
skill's own content.

## 1. Where "Finance / Investment" sits among case types

A 48-hour business case can land in several different problem families. A Finance/Investment case is
specifically about *reading how a company's own numbers behave, and/or deciding whether a specific capital
decision pays off* — not about a deal with another company, changing brand perception, fixing today's supply
chain, or launching a new product.

| Case type | Core question | Primary tool |
|---|---|---|
| **Finance / Investment** | Is this business financially healthy, and is this specific investment (build it ourselves, joint-venture, or acquire) worth the capital it needs? | P&L read (Financial Performance → Revenue Breakdown → Cost Structure) → Payback Period / Investment Value / ROIC |
| Brand Communication | How do we change perception/behaviour toward a brand through a campaign or message? | Issue → Objective → SoG → Winning Point → J2BD → Insight → Idea → IMC → Trade → KPI |
| Profit Optimization | Why is profit falling (or would a proposed move be profitable), and what fixes it? | Profit tree (Revenue − Cost, MECE by industry) → branch analysis → root cause → recommendation |
| Supply Chain / Operations | Why can't the chain deliver goods/materials reliably or efficiently, and how do we fix it? | Demand/Inventory/Supply diagnostic → qualitative (disruption) or quantitative (optimization) solving path |
| M&A / Partnership | Should we buy, merge with, invest in, or partner with another company, and on what terms? | Porter's Five Forces (objective) → SWOT/Strategic Fit → Synergy tree → DCF valuation → go/no-go |
| New Product Launch | Should we launch this new product/feature, what should it look like, and how do we bring it to market? | STP → Whitespace × Strength positioning → Proposition/Positioning → 6Ps go-to-market → revenue/P&L goal |
| Market Entry / Expansion | Should we enter a new market/segment/format, and how? | 3Cs + Market + Financials → Market Sizing → entry-mode fit → go/no-go |
| Growth Strategy | Where should the next unit of growth come from? | Ansoff matrix, portfolio/SoG-style analysis |
| Turnaround / Crisis | How do we stop the bleeding and stabilise after a shock? | Root-cause diagnosis, stakeholder triage, phased plan |
| Pricing | Is the price right, and how should it change? | Elasticity, competitor benchmarking, value-based pricing |

A live brief is rarely a pure type. "Should we acquire this company" could be a Finance/Investment case (the
deliverable is a payback/ROIC number comparing this deal's economics against building the same capability
organically) or an M&A case (the deliverable is a go/no-go and price on this *specific* target, with a strategic-
fit story). "Should we enter this new market" could be Market Entry (the deliverable is a qualitative go/no-go on
the market itself) or Finance/Investment (the deliverable is a payback/ROI number on the capital the entry
requires, with the market choice already settled). The checklist below is how the team tells which one it's
actually holding.

## 2. Checklist — is this a Finance/Investment case?

Run this in the first 15-20 minutes after the brief drops, out loud, as a group. Score it loosely — the more
left-column boxes get ticked, the more confidently the team can commit to this framework.

**Signals it IS a Finance/Investment case**
- The brief hands over multi-year (or multi-period) P&L-style financial data for one company and asks the team
  to evaluate its financial performance, trend, or health — not to diagnose and fix a specific profit problem.
- The brief describes a specific capital-allocation decision — building new production capacity, expanding into
  a new project, or entering via primary investment/joint-venture/acquisition — and the ask is explicitly
  whether it's financially worth it.
- The deliverable is framed around Payback Period, ROI/ROIC, an investment/valuation figure, or a forward-
  looking multi-year P&L build for a not-yet-existing investment or product line.
- The ask compares the *economics* of different ways to pursue the same capital decision (build it ourselves vs.
  joint-venture vs. acquire) purely on the numbers, without one specific acquisition target's own strategic-fit
  story being the point.
- Numbers are central, detailed, and multi-period — revenue, cost, margin, investment cost, discount rate,
  contract length, given or clearly derivable from the brief.

**Signals it's something else (redirect to the matching skill)**
- The ask is "why is profit falling, find the root cause and fix it" on the *existing* business, with no new
  investment or capital decision in view → Profit Optimization.
- A specific acquisition target is already named, and the ask centers on that target's strategic fit, synergy
  story, and a go/no-go/price call on that one deal → M&A; a Finance/Investment case may *compare* the
  economics of build-vs-buy in general, but doesn't build out one target's own SWOT and negotiation story.
- The ask is "should we enter this new market/geography," and the real test is a qualitative judgment on market
  attractiveness, customer fit, and entry-mode choice — not a quantified investment-economics comparison →
  Market Entry; a Finance/Investment case may reuse a market-entry-flavored setup (e.g. "which country should
  we expand into") but the actual test is the Payback/ROIC math, not the market judgment call.
- The ask centers on building a new product concept — positioning, target segment, go-to-market plan — with the
  financial projection as a supporting final step, not the actual test → New Product Launch.

**When it's mixed:** many real prompts blend types — e.g. a market-entry case that narrows down to "and
specifically, is the required investment worth it on the numbers," or an M&A case where the real test is
"compare this acquisition's economics against building the same capability ourselves" rather than a strategic-
fit story on one named target. Scope this framework to the specific sub-question that's actually about reading
the numbers or appraising the investment — and hand the rest to whichever skill matches the broader strategic
question.

## 3. How this fits with other case-type skills

**If `case-orchestrator` is loaded, it now owns this logic centrally.** It reads the whole brief, segments it
into sub-questions, classifies each one against every case-type skill's checklist, and integrates every
sub-framework's output into one coherent case structure tied to a shared objective. Load `case-orchestrator`
first and let it hand off to this skill for any confirmed Finance/Investment sub-question, rather than
re-deriving the classification here. The rest of this section is this skill's own standalone fallback logic, for
when `case-orchestrator` isn't loaded and this skill is being used on its own.

The plan is for this skill to sit alongside siblings for other case types (brand communication, profit
optimization, supply chain, M&A, product launch, market entry, growth strategy, turnaround, pricing, etc.), each
with its own checklist and framework. When more than one is available:

1. **Run every available checklist against the brief before committing to one.** Real briefs blend types more
   often than they're pure.
2. **If exactly one lens fires strongly**, use that skill's framework as the backbone of the whole answer.
3. **If more than one lens fires (a mixed case)**, don't let each framework run in isolation and get stapled
   together at the end. Nominate one owner for the shared throughline — one SCQ (Situation-Complication-Question)
   and one overarching Objective that every sub-framework's output has to tie back to. Each specialized section
   should open by naming which sub-question it's answering and close by connecting back to that shared
   Objective, so the final answer reads as one solution arrived at from multiple angles, not several
   disconnected mini-decks.
4. **If nothing fires clearly**, don't force this framework. Say so, and fall back to general business-case
   structuring (root cause → objective → options → recommendation) rather than distorting the case to fit a
   template that doesn't match.

**Specifically with `case-profit-optimization`:** this skill's Financial Performance / Revenue Breakdown / Cost
Structure steps look similar to a profit tree — but the test for which skill owns the sub-question is what the
final recommendation looks like. A profit-optimization case wants a diagnosed root cause on the *existing*
business and a fix; a Finance/Investment case wants a financial-health read that feeds into a forward-looking
capital decision (or a standalone payback/ROIC appraisal of a not-yet-made investment). "Why did our margin
shrink last year" is Profit Optimization; "is our financial performance strong enough to support the new
investment we're considering" is Finance/Investment.

**Specifically with `case-ma`:** the two skills share the same underlying math family — this skill's Payback
Period / Investment Value / ROIC formulas are the light, general-purpose version of `case-ma`'s Synergy tree and
DCF valuation. If the brief has already committed to one specific, named acquisition target and the test is that
target's strategic fit and a negotiated price, that's `case-ma`. If the brief is comparing *how* to pursue a
capital decision in general (build it ourselves vs. joint-venture vs. acquire, purely on the numbers, without one
target's own SWOT story) or asking whether the underlying business is financially healthy enough to invest at
all, that's `case-investment`.

**Specifically with `case-market-entry`:** a market-entry-flavored setup ("which country should we expand into")
can still be a Finance/Investment case if the actual test is the Payback/ROIC math on the entry investment, not a
qualitative judgment on which market is the best strategic fit. `case-market-entry`'s own Financials pillar uses
a lighter version of this skill's investment-appraisal formulas for a quick gut-check — hand off to this skill
in full when the investment appraisal itself, not the market choice, is what's actually being tested.

As sibling skills get added, keep them in the same `case-<type>` naming pattern so they're easy to spot as one
family.

## 4. The framework, step by step

A Finance/Investment case draws on two related but distinct tools, and a given brief may need one or both:

- **A. Financial Performance Read** — for when the brief hands over a company's own historical P&L and asks how
  healthy its performance actually is.
- **B. Investment Appraisal** — for when the brief describes a specific capital decision (build it ourselves,
  joint-venture, or acquire) and asks whether it's financially worth pursuing.

Decide which (or both) the brief is actually asking for in the first few minutes, right after the checklist
confirms this case type. The roadmap below is a summary. This public copy does not include the full worked
detail — the complete P&L structure and ratio-analysis method, or the entry-method comparison table — or worked
examples (kept out deliberately, see the repo README) — read it as a checklist and step outline, not a complete
how-to.

**A. Financial Performance Read — four steps, each building on the one before it.**
1. **Financial Performance** — read the topline P&L (sales volume, gross revenue, COGS, gross profit, NOPBT)
   across the periods given, year over year, to establish the headline story before drilling into any one line.
2. **Revenue Breakdown** — decompose total revenue by product line, customer segment, channel, and geography;
   look for which lines are growing vs. shrinking and whether there's a seasonal pattern worth planning around.
3. **Cost Structure Analysis** — break down product cost, COGS components, and operating-expense components,
   each against its own trend and as a share of revenue, to isolate exactly which cost line is moving and why.
4. **Recommendation** — land on both levers together: how to grow revenue (more volume, or a higher average
   price via product mix) and how to cut cost (operating-expense efficiency, COGS/input-cost efficiency).

**B. Investment Appraisal — for a specific capital decision.**
1. **Establish the investment's basic economics** — revenue the investment would generate, the operating-
   expense ratio it would run at, and the upfront capital it requires.
2. **Compute Annual Profit** = Revenue × (1 − Opex%), adding a synergy-revenue term if the decision is an
   acquisition with expected synergies (the same logic as `case-ma`'s synergy tree).
3. **Compute Payback Period** = Initial Investment / Annual Profit — how many years until the investment pays
   for itself.
4. **Compute Investment Value** = Annual Profit / Discount Rate — a perpetuity-style shortcut for the investment's
   worth, useful under case-time pressure; if the brief instead hands over multi-year projections rather than one
   flat annual figure, discount each year's cash flow properly instead of using this shortcut.
5. **Compute ROIC** = (Investment Value / Initial Investment) − 1 — a positive, healthy ROIC marks a "value
   creator"; a near-zero or negative one marks a "value destroyer."
6. **If comparing entry methods** (organic/primary investment vs. joint venture vs. acquisition), run steps 2-5
   for each option and compare Payback Period and ROIC side by side, while also naming the qualitative
   trade-offs (control, speed, local-market experience, integration risk) that the raw numbers don't capture —
   the best-ROIC option isn't automatically the right recommendation if it also carries much higher execution
   risk or takes far longer to stand up.

Worked examples are not included in this public copy.

## 5. What information each step needs

If the checklist confirms a Finance/Investment case (in full or in part), here's what the framework asks for at
each step. This list is deliberately generic — it names the *kind* of data each step needs, not a specific
team's files, so it stays valid regardless of which company or competition it's applied to.

| Framework step | Data it needs |
|---|---|
| Financial Performance | Multi-period revenue, COGS, gross profit, and NOPBT (or enough line items to derive them) |
| Revenue Breakdown | Revenue split by product/segment/channel/geography, ideally with more than one period to show trend |
| Cost Structure Analysis | Cost line items (raw material, packaging, manufacturing, warehouse, transportation, selling, marketing, admin) with enough periods to show trend |
| Investment Appraisal (Annual Profit, Payback, Investment Value, ROIC) | Projected or given revenue for the investment, an opex ratio or cost structure, the upfront investment amount, and a discount rate (case briefs almost always hand over the discount rate directly — it's rarely something to derive) |

**Before the clock starts on an actual case, check your own team's prep against this list** — for each row, do
you already have this data, or will it have to come live from the brief? This is a heavier-arithmetic case type:
the P&L structure and the four investment-appraisal formulas are reusable regardless of company, but a real
Finance/Investment case usually leans hard on being comfortable doing the arithmetic quickly and correctly under
time pressure, so drilling the P&L waterfall and the Payback/Investment-Value/ROIC formulas until they're second
nature matters more here than for a mostly-qualitative case type.

If your team keeps a research index for the current competition (which company, which files, which framework
step each one covers), treat it as a separate document alongside this skill, not part of it — that keeps this
skill reusable for a future competition without editing, and keeps this skill's *classification logic*
independent of any one team's research, which may be incomplete or unverified in places.

## 6. The case-solving process, if it's a Finance/Investment case

A suggested run of show once the checklist confirms this case type and the clock starts. Rescale the timing to
the actual deadline, but keep the order — each step depends on the one before it. If the brief only asks for one
of the two tools (Financial Performance Read or Investment Appraisal), skip the other half and reallocate its
time to depth on the half that's actually being tested.

| Time (of ~10 working hrs) | Step | What the team does |
|---|---|---|
| First 20 min | Confirm case type | Run the Section 2 checklist as a group. Agree explicitly: "this is a Finance/Investment case, asking for [financial performance read / investment appraisal / both]." Assign roles (P&L/Revenue lead, Cost Structure lead, Investment-Math lead, Risk/QA lead). |
| 20-60 min | SCQ + Financial Performance | Write the SCQ. Read the topline P&L across all periods given and land on the headline story (growing/shrinking, driven by volume or price, margin expanding or compressing). |
| 60-150 min | Revenue Breakdown + Cost Structure | Decompose revenue by product/segment/channel/geography; decompose cost by line item. Identify which specific lines are driving the headline story from the prior step. |
| 150-270 min | Investment Appraisal (if applicable) | Compute Annual Profit, Payback Period, Investment Value, and ROIC for the capital decision in question; if comparing entry methods, run the same math for each option. |
| 270-360 min | Recommendation | Land on the growth/cost levers (Financial Performance Read) and/or the go/no-go and preferred method (Investment Appraisal), explicitly tied back to the Step 1 SCQ. |
| 360-420 min | Risk / stress-test | Stress-test the recommendation against a changed assumption (what if the discount rate or opex ratio moves, what if projected revenue undershoots). Name the top 1-2 risks explicitly rather than presenting the numbers as certain. |
| Remaining time | Synthesis & rehearsal | Compress onto one cheat-sheet page: the headline financial story, the key revenue/cost drivers, the investment math (Payback/ROIC), the recommendation, the top 1-2 risks. Rehearse the arc: Financial Performance → Drivers → Investment Math → Recommendation. Time-box Q&A prep — expect challenges on the discount-rate and opex assumptions, and on how the numbers compare against the qualitative trade-offs of each option. |

## 7. Common failure modes to avoid

- **Presenting a financial-performance read with no headline story.** A wall of YoY percentages with no clear
  "here's what's actually happening and why" narrative reads as data dumping, not analysis — always lead with
  the headline before drilling into the components.
- **Building an investment appraisal without stating the discount-rate and opex assumptions out loud.** These
  two inputs drive the whole Payback/Investment-Value/ROIC output; a case that glosses over where they came from
  invites the sharpest Q&A pushback, the same failure mode `case-ma` flags for its own DCF assumptions.
- **Treating the Investment Value perpetuity shortcut as always valid.** It assumes the investment's annual
  profit continues unchanged forever — fine as a fast estimate under time pressure, but if the brief hands over
  multi-year projections that clearly change over time, use those directly rather than forcing the shortcut.
- **Picking the highest-ROIC option without naming what the number doesn't capture.** Control, speed to market,
  local-market experience, and integration risk all matter and don't show up in a raw ROIC comparison — a
  recommendation that's silent on these reads as less rigorous than one that names them explicitly.
- **Confusing a financial-health read with a root-cause diagnosis.** If the case is really "why did profit fall
  and what fixes it" with no new investment in view, that's `case-profit-optimization`, not this skill — don't
  force an investment-appraisal frame onto a case that isn't actually about a new capital decision.
- **Running the full Investment Appraisal when the brief only asked for the Financial Performance Read (or vice
  versa).** Building out the half that wasn't actually asked for wastes the clock and dilutes the answer's depth
  exactly where it should be deepest.

## 8. One-page decision flow (first few minutes)

1. Read the brief once, cover to cover, before discussing — don't let the first person's guess anchor the room.
2. Underline every mention of multi-period financial data, a specific investment/capital decision, "payback,"
   "ROI/ROIC," or "should we invest," vs. a root-cause profit-decline ask on the existing business, a named
   acquisition target's strategic fit, or a qualitative market-entry judgment call.
3. Score the case against the Section 2 checklist as a group — out loud, fast, don't overthink individual boxes.
4. If it's a Finance/Investment case: decide whether it needs the Financial Performance Read, the Investment
   Appraisal, or both; open your team's prepped P&L/investment-math muscle memory, assign roles, start the
   Section 6 timeline.
5. If it's mixed: agree which sub-question gets this treatment and which gets a different framework (see
   Section 3), and say so explicitly in the final answer.
6. If it's clearly something else entirely: set this skill aside and use the matching case-type skill instead.
