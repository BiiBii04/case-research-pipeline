# New Product Launch Case — Checklist and Framework

This skill turns a case-solving method into something usable live, during a competition, under
time pressure. It is one member of a family of case-type skills (`case-brand-communication`,
`case-profit-optimization`, `case-supply-chain`, `case-ma`, `case-product-launch`, and later `case-market-entry`,
`case-turnaround`, `case-pricing`, and others) that together cover the case types a team might draw. Read the
"How this fits with other case-type skills" section below first if more than one case-type skill is loaded — it
explains how to keep the final answer coherent instead of stapling together separate mini-answers.

The content here is an original, rewritten summary of standard product-marketing and go-to-market methods (STP,
positioning theory, the marketing mix, customer-journey mapping, revenue/P&L goal-setting). Keep this skill
team-internal; don't redistribute outside the competition team.

**By design, this skill is company-agnostic.** It knows how to recognise a product-launch case and how to solve
one — it does not know anything about any specific company or product, real or fictional, and doesn't reference
any particular team's research files by name. That's deliberate: the checklist and framework need to work
unchanged on whatever brief actually drops, for this competition or a future one, without being tuned (or
biased) toward one team's prep material. If your team has built company-specific research for the current
competition, keep the pointer to it in a separate, clearly-labelled file outside this skill (see Section 5) —
don't fold it into this skill's own content.

## 1. Where "Product Launch" sits among case types

A 48-hour business case can land in several different problem families. A Product Launch case is specifically
about *introducing something new to the market* — a new product, a re-launch of an existing one, or a new
feature on an existing product/app — not about a deal with another company, fixing today's supply chain, or
diagnosing why today's existing business is or isn't profitable.

| Case type | Core question | Primary tool |
|---|---|---|
| **Product Launch** | Should we launch this new product/feature, what should it look like, and how do we bring it to market? | STP → Whitespace × Strength positioning → Proposition/Positioning → 6Ps go-to-market → revenue/P&L goal |
| Brand Communication | How do we change perception/behaviour toward a brand through a campaign or message? | Issue → Objective → SoG → Winning Point → J2BD → Insight → Idea → IMC → Trade → KPI |
| Profit Optimization | Why is profit falling (or would a proposed move be profitable), and what fixes it? | Profit tree (Revenue − Cost, MECE by industry) → branch analysis → root cause → recommendation |
| Supply Chain / Operations | Why can't the chain deliver goods/materials reliably or efficiently, and how do we fix it? | Demand/Inventory/Supply diagnostic → qualitative (disruption) or quantitative (optimization) solving path |
| M&A / Partnership | Should we buy, merge with, invest in, or partner with another company, and on what terms? | Porter's Five Forces (objective) → SWOT/Strategic Fit → Synergy tree → DCF valuation → go/no-go |
| Market Entry / Expansion | Should we enter a new market/segment/format, and how? | TAM/SAM/SOM, entry-mode analysis, go/no-go criteria |
| Growth Strategy | Where should the next unit of growth come from? | Ansoff matrix, portfolio/SoG-style analysis |
| Turnaround / Crisis | How do we stop the bleeding and stabilise after a shock? | Root-cause diagnosis, stakeholder triage, phased plan |
| Pricing | Is the price right, and how should it change? | Elasticity, competitor benchmarking, value-based pricing |

A live brief is rarely a pure type. "Should we enter this new market" could be a Market Entry case (the
deliverable is a go/no-go on the market itself) or a Product Launch case (if the real question is "what new,
locally-adapted product should we build to enter it" — the market choice is a given, and the launch mechanics are
the actual test). "Should we acquire this startup's product line" could be an M&A case (the deliverable is a
valuation and deal terms) or a Product Launch case wearing an acquisition costume (if the real question is "what
should this new product look like and how do we bring it to market," and the acquisition is just how the IP
changed hands). The checklist below is how the team tells which one it's actually holding.

## 2. Checklist — is this a Product Launch case?

Run this in the first 15-20 minutes after the brief drops, out loud, as a group. Score it loosely — the more
left-column boxes get ticked, the more confidently the team can commit to this framework.

**Signals it IS a Product Launch case**
- The brief describes a company introducing something new: a brand-new product, a re-launch/improvement of an
  existing one, or a new feature on an existing product/app/service.
- The ask is about *whether* to launch, *what* the product/feature should be, or *how* to bring it to market —
  not a deal with a second company, a cost diagnosis of the existing business, or a perception/behaviour
  campaign for something already on the market.
- The brief gives (or explicitly asks the team to identify) an unmet customer need or pain point the new
  product is meant to solve.
- The deliverable sounds like "should we launch this, what should it look like, who's it for, and how do we take
  it to market" — a Product Concept plus a go-to-market plan, not a valuation, a root-cause diagnosis, or a
  campaign idea for an existing product.
- A revenue or P&L goal-setting ask is tied specifically to the *new* product/feature, not the existing
  business's overall profit trend.

**Signals it's something else (redirect to the matching skill)**
- The ask is "is our current business profitable / what's dragging profit down," with no new product or feature
  in view → Profit Optimization.
- The ask is "should we enter a new geography/segment," and the *market* choice is the actual deliverable, with
  any new-product detail left generic or secondary → Market Entry; a Product Launch case has already settled on
  a specific market and is now asking what to build and how to launch it there.
- The ask is "should we buy/merge with/invest in another company," even if that company's product is what's
  ultimately being "launched" under the acquirer → M&A; the deal terms and valuation are the actual test, not
  the go-to-market mechanics.
- The ask is about shifting perception or behaviour toward a brand or product that's *already on the market*, via
  a campaign or message, with no new product/feature actually being introduced → Brand Communication.
- A supply/operations problem is described, with no new product in view → Supply Chain.

**When it's mixed:** many real prompts blend types — e.g. a market-entry case that narrows down to "and
specifically, what product should we launch to enter this market," or a brand-communication case that's really
about introducing a new product line under an existing, already-positioned brand (in which case the new
product's Proposition needs to stay consistent with that brand's existing Positioning, not contradict it). Scope
this framework to the specific sub-question that's
actually about the new product — what it is, who it's for, how it reaches the market — and hand the rest to
whichever skill matches the broader strategic question.

## 3. How this fits with other case-type skills

**If `case-orchestrator` is loaded, it now owns this logic centrally.** It reads the whole brief, segments it
into sub-questions, classifies each one against every case-type skill's checklist, and integrates every
sub-framework's output into one coherent case structure tied to a shared objective. Load `case-orchestrator`
first and let it hand off to this skill for any confirmed Product Launch sub-question, rather than re-deriving
the classification here. The rest of this section is this skill's own standalone fallback logic, for when
`case-orchestrator` isn't loaded and this skill is being used on its own.

The plan is for this skill to sit alongside siblings for other case types (brand communication, profit
optimization, supply chain, M&A, market entry, pricing, turnaround, etc.), each with its own checklist and
framework. When more than one is available:

1. **Run every available checklist against the brief before committing to one.** Real briefs blend types more
   often than they're pure.
2. **If exactly one lens fires strongly**, use that skill's framework as the backbone of the whole answer.
3. **If more than one lens fires (a mixed case)**, don't let each framework run in isolation and get stapled
   together at the end. Nominate one owner for the shared throughline — one SCQ (Situation-Complication-Question)
   and one overarching Objective that every sub-framework's output has to tie back to. Each specialized section
   should open by naming which sub-question it's answering and close by connecting back to that shared
   Objective, so the final answer reads as one solution arrived at from multiple angles, not several
   disconnected mini-decks.
4. **If nothing fires clearly**, don't force this framework. Say so, and fall back to general business-case
   structuring (root cause → objective → options → recommendation) rather than distorting the case to fit a
   template that doesn't match.

**Specifically with `case-brand-communication`:** a genuinely new product's launch still needs a Proposition,
which is a narrower, more product-specific cousin of a full brand Positioning — but
the test for which skill owns the sub-question is what's actually being introduced. If a *new product* is
launching, this skill owns the Proposition/go-to-market work (even though its Promotion piece borrows
communication-mix thinking). If the brief is about changing how customers feel about an *existing* product or
brand with nothing new being introduced, that's Brand Communication instead.

**Specifically with `case-profit-optimization`:** this skill's Step 4 (Financial Analysis) builds a revenue/P&L
projection for the new product, which can look similar to a profit tree — but a profit-optimization case is
diagnosing *why the existing business's* profit is what it is, while this skill is *projecting* what a
not-yet-existing product's revenue/cost/profit would look like. "Will this new product be profitable enough to
launch" is a Product Launch case whose Step 4 happens to use profit-tree-style arithmetic, not a
profit-optimization case that happens to mention a new product.

**Specifically with `case-market-entry` (not yet built):** when a brief combines "enter this new market" with
"and here's the specific product we'd bring," decide whether the market-entry decision or the product itself is
the actual test being scored. If the market choice is still open, that's Market Entry with this skill's Product
Development/Strategy-to-Win steps feeding into it as supporting detail; if the market is already a given and the
brief is really asking what to build and how to launch it there, this skill owns the sub-question outright.

As sibling skills get added, keep them in the same `case-<type>` naming pattern so they're easy to spot as one
family.

## 4. The framework, step by step

Unlike some other case types, a Product Launch case doesn't split into a "long" vs. "short/interview" format in
the source material this skill is built from — the same four-step roadmap applies whether the team has a full
multi-hour build or a compressed interview window; a shorter format just means moving faster through each step
and leaning harder on the team's own judgment where the brief doesn't hand over enough data.

The roadmap below is a summary. This public copy does not include the full worked detail for every step — the
High-/Low-Involvement lens, the STP model and persona-building, the Whitespace × Strength tool, the
Proposition/Positioning theory, the 6Ps-to-touchpoints mapping, the Place/Promotion mechanics, or the
revenue-goal/P&L formulas — or worked examples (kept out deliberately, see the repo README) — read it as a
checklist and step outline, not a complete how-to.

**Step 1 — Issues & Opportunities ("should we launch this product?").** Open with an SCQ, then answer three
questions: what customer *Unmet Need or Pain point* does the product need to solve; is the *Market size &
growth* big enough and rising; and who/what *Competitors* already occupy this space. Before digging in, check
which side of the High-Involvement/Low-Involvement line the industry sits on — it reshapes how every later step
should be approached, from how much market education the launch needs to which channels actually matter.

**Step 2 — Product Development ("how will the product solve the customer's problem?").** Run STP to land on a
target segment and persona, use the Whitespace × Strength tool to find the open positioning space, then build
the Product Concept: a Proposition stating what need it serves, for whom, how it's differentiated, and why
that's believable; the specific product design/features that solve the identified pain point; and a USP that's
genuinely defensible against what competitors already offer.

**Step 3 — Strategy to Win (the go-to-market plan).** Build the 6Ps marketing mix — Price, Promotion, Place,
Physical evidence, People, Packaging — mapped onto the three customer touchpoints (Communication, In-store,
In-use). For a launch specifically, Promotion and Place are where most of the answer's weight should go; Price
and Packaging are secondary/supplementary detail unless the brief hands over enough cost data to make Price the
centerpiece.

**Step 4 — Financial Analysis.** Set a revenue goal using whichever of the three goal-setting methods best fits
the industry, build a three-line P&L (Turnover → Gross Profit → Net Profit before overheads), and flag the
launch's key financial risks.

Worked examples are not included in this public copy.

## 5. What information each step needs

If the checklist confirms a Product Launch case (in full or in part), here's what the framework asks for at each
step. This list is deliberately generic — it names the *kind* of data each step needs, not a specific team's
files, so it stays valid regardless of which company or competition it's applied to.

| Framework step | Data it needs |
|---|---|
| Issues & Opportunities | Category/market-size and growth-rate figures; any customer research (surveys, interviews) pointing at an unmet need; a rough competitor landscape for the category |
| Product Development (STP, Whitespace × Strength, Proposition) | Whatever the brief states or implies about potential customer segments and their behaviour/psychology; a read on the brand's own strongest capability, to plot against competitor strengths |
| Strategy to Win (6Ps) | The target customer's actual shopping/media habits (to pick Place/Promotion channels); the company's existing distribution footprint if this is a launch under an existing brand, rather than a wholly new entrant |
| Financial Analysis (revenue goal + P&L) | Total addressable households or outlets for the category (for the goal-setting method chosen); unit price, trade-expense, COGS, and marketing-cost assumptions, either given or built from stated industry norms |

**Before the clock starts on an actual case, check your own team's prep against this list** — for each row, do
you already have this data, or will it have to come live from the brief? This is a comparatively qualitative
case type: the STP/Whitespace/6Ps mechanics are the reusable muscle memory worth drilling, while the actual
numbers (market size, revenue goal, P&L) are usually built live off whatever the brief hands over, so drilling
the *structure* of each step matters more here than memorizing formulas.

If your team keeps a research index for the current competition (which company, which files, which framework
step each one covers), treat it as a separate document alongside this skill, not part of it — that keeps this
skill reusable for a future competition without editing, and keeps this skill's *classification logic*
independent of any one team's research, which may be incomplete or unverified in places.

## 6. The case-solving process, if it's a Product Launch case

A suggested run of show once the checklist confirms this case type and the clock starts. Rescale the timing to
the actual deadline, but keep the order — each step depends on the one before it.

| Time (of ~10 working hrs) | Step | What the team does |
|---|---|---|
| First 20 min | Confirm case type | Run the Section 2 checklist as a group. Agree explicitly: "this is a Product Launch case, for [product/feature] in [category]." Assign roles (Research/Insight lead, STP & Positioning lead, Go-to-Market lead, Financial lead). |
| 20-60 min | SCQ + Issues & Opportunities | Write the SCQ. Establish the unmet need, market size/growth, and competitive landscape; place the category on the High-/Low-Involvement spectrum to set the tone for every later step. |
| 60-180 min | Product Development | Run STP through to a named target persona (4W1H). Plot the Whitespace × Strength grid to find the open position. Write the Proposition Statement and lock the USP. |
| 180-300 min | Strategy to Win | Build the 6Ps plan. Map the customer journey and pick the Place channels matched to the target persona's actual shopping habits; for a genuine new launch, prioritize Awareness in the Promotion plan and design the two-stage (Massive Launch → Sustain Reach) campaign structure if a full campaign build is expected. |
| 300-390 min | Financial Analysis | Pick the revenue-goal method that fits the category, build the three-line P&L, and flag the top financial risks (assumption sensitivity, cost overruns). |
| 390-450 min | Recommendation | Draft the final recommendation, explicitly tied back to the Step 1 unmet need and market opportunity — not just "the P&L works," but "this is why customers will actually want this." |
| Remaining time | Synthesis & rehearsal | Compress onto one cheat-sheet page: the unmet need, the Proposition, the headline go-to-market moves, the revenue goal, the top 1-2 risks. Rehearse the arc: Opportunity → Product Concept → Go-to-Market → Financials → recommendation. Time-box Q&A prep — expect challenges on the target-segment choice, the USP's real defensibility, and the revenue-goal assumptions. |

## 7. Common failure modes to avoid

- **Building a Proposition without first nailing the unmet need.** A product concept that isn't traceable back
  to a specific, evidenced customer pain point reads as a feature list, not a case answer — Step 2 has to visibly
  build on Step 1's finding, not restart from a generic "what would be cool to build."
- **Picking a target segment that isn't actually well-matched.** The STP model's own emphasis: a wrong Targeting
  call undermines everything built on top of it — a persona chosen for convenience rather than fit (best profit
  potential, growth capacity, strategic alignment) weakens the whole rest of the answer.
- **Claiming a Winning Point that's really a Losing Point.** A USP that sits where competitors are already
  strongest isn't a whitespace claim — it's picking a fight from behind. Plot the Whitespace × Strength grid
  honestly rather than asserting differentiation without checking it against where competitors actually are.
- **Treating Price and Packaging as equally important as Promotion and Place.** For a launch specifically, the
  source framework is explicit that Promotion and Place carry the most weight — spending equal analytical time
  on all six Ps dilutes the answer's depth exactly where it should be deepest.
- **Ignoring the High-/Low-Involvement distinction.** Applying a Low-Involvement launch's fast, sampling-heavy,
  emotionally-driven playbook to a High-Involvement product (or vice versa) misreads what actually drives that
  category's purchase decision, and the go-to-market plan built on the wrong assumption won't hold up under Q&A.
- **Confusing a New Feature launch with a full New Product launch.** A new feature on an existing app doesn't
  need a from-scratch brand Positioning, a new pricing model, or new-channel-building — using the full
  new-product treatment on a feature-launch brief over-builds the answer and misses that the real lever is
  usually in-app UX and owned-channel promotion, not a full campaign.
- **Setting a revenue goal that doesn't trace to a stated method or assumption.** An unsupported "$X million in
  year one" reads as a guess; picking one of the three goal-setting methods and showing the inputs (market size,
  penetration rate, distribution coverage) makes the number defensible under Q&A.

## 8. One-page decision flow (first few minutes)

1. Read the brief once, cover to cover, before discussing — don't let the first person's guess anchor the room.
2. Underline every mention of a new product, a re-launch, a new feature, "should we introduce/launch," an unmet
   customer need, or a go-to-market ask, vs. a deal with a second company, a pure internal cost-diagnosis on the
   existing business, or a perception/behaviour-change ask for something already on the market.
3. Score the case against the Section 2 checklist as a group — out loud, fast, don't overthink individual boxes.
4. If it's a Product Launch case: check whether it's a full new product or a new-feature-on-an-existing-product
   variant, open your team's prepped STP/positioning/6Ps muscle memory, assign roles, start the Section 6
   timeline.
5. If it's mixed: agree which sub-question gets this treatment and which gets a different framework (see
   Section 3), and say so explicitly in the final answer.
6. If it's clearly something else entirely: set this skill aside and use the matching case-type skill instead.
