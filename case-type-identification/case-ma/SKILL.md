# M&A Case — Checklist and Framework

This skill turns a case-solving method into something usable live, during a competition, under
time pressure. It is one member of a family of case-type skills (`case-brand-communication`,
`case-profit-optimization`, `case-supply-chain`, `case-ma`, and later `case-market-entry`, `case-product-launch`,
and others) that together cover the case types a team might draw. Read the "How this fits with other case-type
skills" section below first if more than one case-type skill is loaded — it explains how to keep the final
answer coherent instead of stapling together separate mini-answers.

The content here is an original, rewritten summary of standard corporate-finance and M&A-strategy methods
(Porter's Five Forces, SWOT/strategic-fit analysis, the synergy-value tree, discounted cash flow valuation,
comparable-company and precedent-transaction analysis, stock vs. asset acquisition structures). Keep this skill
team-internal; don't redistribute outside the competition team.

**By design, this skill is company-agnostic.** It knows how to recognise an M&A case and how to solve one — it
does not know anything about any specific company, real or fictional, and doesn't reference any particular
team's research files by name. That's deliberate: the checklist and framework need to work unchanged on whatever
brief actually drops, for this competition or a future one, without being tuned (or biased) toward one team's
prep material. If your team has built company-specific research for the current competition, keep the pointer to
it in a separate, clearly-labelled file outside this skill (see Section 5) — don't fold it into this skill's own
content.

## 1. Where "M&A" sits among case types

A 48-hour business case can land in several different problem families. An M&A case is specifically about
*whether and how to combine with, invest in, or acquire another company* — not about fixing a supply chain,
changing brand perception, or checking whether today's business as-is is profitable.

| Case type | Core question | Primary tool |
|---|---|---|
| **M&A / Partnership** | Should we buy, merge with, invest in, or partner with another company, and on what terms? | Porter's Five Forces (objective) → SWOT/Strategic Fit → Synergy tree → DCF valuation → go/no-go |
| Brand Communication | How do we change perception/behaviour toward a brand through a campaign or message? | Issue → Objective → SoG → Winning Point → J2BD → Insight → Idea → IMC → Trade → KPI |
| Profit Optimization | Why is profit falling (or would a proposed move be profitable), and what fixes it? | Profit tree (Revenue − Cost, MECE by industry) → branch analysis → root cause → recommendation |
| Supply Chain / Operations | Why can't the chain deliver goods/materials reliably or efficiently, and how do we fix it? | Demand/Inventory/Supply diagnostic → qualitative (disruption) or quantitative (optimization) solving path |
| Market Entry / Expansion | Should we enter a new market/segment/format, and how? | TAM/SAM/SOM, entry-mode analysis, go/no-go criteria |
| Growth Strategy | Where should the next unit of growth come from? | Ansoff matrix, portfolio/SoG-style analysis |
| Turnaround / Crisis | How do we stop the bleeding and stabilise after a shock? | Root-cause diagnosis, stakeholder triage, phased plan |
| Pricing | Is the price right, and how should it change? | Elasticity, competitor benchmarking, value-based pricing |

A live brief is rarely a pure type. "Should we acquire this supplier" could be an M&A case (the deliverable is a
go/no-go and a price), a supply-chain case (if the real question is "how do we secure this input, and buying the
supplier is one option among several"), or a profit-optimization case (if the real question is "is this
purchase worth it on a pure cost-benefit basis, independent of any strategic-fit story"). The checklist below is
how the team tells which one it's actually holding.

## 2. Checklist — is this an M&A case?

Run this in the first 15-20 minutes after the brief drops, out loud, as a group. Score it loosely — the more
left-column boxes get ticked, the more confidently the team can commit to this framework.

**Signals it IS an M&A case**
- The brief names two companies (or a company and an unnamed "target" or "candidate") and asks whether one
  should buy, merge with, invest in, or partner with the other.
- The ask is explicitly about deal terms — a purchase price, a valuation, an ROI/payback target, or "is this
  deal worth it."
- The brief gives financial or operating data for *both* companies (revenue, growth rate, cost structure,
  market position) rather than just one.
- The deliverable it's fishing for sounds like "should we do this deal, and at what price/terms," not a
  campaign, a cost diagnosis on the existing business, or a market-entry recommendation.
- A financial-buyer angle is present — a private equity or investment fund evaluating an acquisition purely for
  return, rather than a strategic acquirer looking for operational fit.
- The brief mentions synergies, strategic fit, due diligence, integration risk, or a specific acquisition
  structure (buying shares vs. buying assets).

**Signals it's something else (redirect to the matching skill)**
- Only one company is in view, and the ask is "is our current business profitable / what's dragging profit
  down" with no second company or deal on the table → Profit Optimization.
- The ask is "should we enter a new geography/segment," and any acquisition mentioned is just one *possible
  entry mode* among several (organic build, joint venture, acquisition) rather than the case's actual focus →
  Market Entry; an M&A case has already settled on "acquire" as the mechanism and is now asking whether/how,
  not weighing entry modes against each other.
- A supply/operations problem is described, and "should we acquire our supplier" is floated as one *possible
  fix* among several operational options, not the deliverable itself → Supply Chain, with the acquisition idea
  as one candidate solution in a decision matrix, not a standalone valuation exercise.
- The brief is really about "where should growth come from" in general, with acquisition as one lever among
  organic growth, new products, and new markets → Growth Strategy; an M&A case has a specific, named target
  already in view.

**When it's mixed:** many real prompts blend types — e.g. a growth-strategy case that narrows down to "and
specifically, should we acquire Company X to get there," or a turnaround case where a distressed company is
itself the acquisition target. Scope this framework to the specific sub-question that's actually about the
deal — the target, the price, the synergy case — and hand the rest to whichever skill matches the broader
strategic question.

## 3. How this fits with other case-type skills

**If `case-orchestrator` is loaded, it now owns this logic centrally.** It reads the whole brief, segments it
into sub-questions, classifies each one against every case-type skill's checklist, and integrates every
sub-framework's output into one coherent case structure tied to a shared objective. Load `case-orchestrator`
first and let it hand off to this skill for any confirmed M&A sub-question, rather than re-deriving the
classification here. The rest of this section is this skill's own standalone fallback logic, for the case
`case-orchestrator` isn't loaded and this skill is being used on its own.

The plan is for this skill to sit alongside siblings for other case types (brand communication, profit
optimization, supply chain, product launch, market entry, pricing, turnaround, etc.), each with its own
checklist and framework. When more than one is available:

1. **Run every available checklist against the brief before committing to one.** Real briefs blend types more
   often than they're pure.
2. **If exactly one lens fires strongly**, use that skill's framework as the backbone of the whole answer.
3. **If more than one lens fires (a mixed case)**, don't let each framework run in isolation and get stapled
   together at the end. Nominate one owner for the shared throughline — one SCQ (Situation-Complication-Question)
   and one overarching Objective that every sub-framework's output has to tie back to. Each specialized section
   should open by naming which sub-question it's answering and close by connecting back to that shared
   Objective, so the final answer reads as one solution arrived at from multiple angles, not several
   disconnected mini-decks.
4. **If nothing fires clearly**, don't force this framework. Say so, and fall back to general business-case
   structuring (root cause → objective → options → recommendation) rather than distorting the case to fit a
   template that doesn't match.

**Specifically with `case-profit-optimization`:** an M&A case's Step 3 (Synergy) shares the same Revenue/Cost
MECE instinct as a profit tree — but the test for which skill owns the sub-question is what the final
recommendation looks like. A profit-optimization case wants a diagnosed root cause on the *existing* business
and a fix; an M&A case wants a go/no-go decision on a *second* company plus a price. "Would combining with
Company X actually make us more profitable" is an M&A case whose evidence happens to be a profit calculation, not
a profit-optimization case that happens to mention another company.

**Specifically with `case-supply-chain`:** when a brief proposes acquiring a supplier or a link in the value
chain, ask whether the deliverable is a valuation and a deal recommendation (M&A) or an operational redesign
where acquisition is one option among several being weighed against, say, multi-sourcing or postponement
(Supply Chain). A vertical-merger scenario can legitimately open with this skill's Porter's-Five-Forces framing
and then hand the "how do we actually run the combined supply chain" piece to `case-supply-chain`.

As sibling skills get added, keep them in the same `case-<type>` naming pattern so they're easy to spot as one
family.

## 4. The framework, step by step

M&A cases show up in two distinct shapes, and — like the qualitative/quantitative split in a supply-chain
case — which shape you're in determines which process to actually run. Decide this in the first few minutes,
right after the checklist confirms an M&A case:

- **Long case (competition format)** — the brief hands over real data for both companies and expects a full,
  multi-hour build: an M&A Objective, a Strategic Fit analysis, a quantified Synergy estimate, and a Valuation
  feasibility check. This is the default assumption for a case competition.
- **Short/interview case** — a compressed, ~30-minute format where much of the data isn't given up front and has
  to be actively drawn out by asking questions, and the answer is judged more on structure and reasoning quality
  than on arithmetic precision.

The roadmap below is a summary. This public copy does not include the full worked detail for either format — the
Strategic Fit checklist, the synergy-value tree, the DCF mechanics, or the four-lens interview framework — or
worked examples (kept out deliberately, see the repo README) — read it as a checklist and step outline, not a
complete how-to.

**A. Long-case format — five steps, each depending on the one before it.**
1. **M&A Objective** — run Porter's Five Forces on the acquirer's industry to establish *why* a deal is strategically
   needed (which competitive force is under pressure, and how the target would relieve it), before touching any
   numbers.
2. **Company Analysis** — SWOT both companies (acquirer and target) to identify Strategic Fit: complementary or
   duplicate products/services, technology/patents, target market and customers, corporate culture, supply chain
   and partners.
3. **Financial Analysis** — estimate the dollar value of Synergy the deal would create, using a Revenue/Cost/
   Capital/Other-Financial MECE tree, grounded in each company's own historical data and industry benchmarks.
4. **Acquisition Feasibility** — value the target (Discounted Cash Flow is the default method) and compare that
   valuation against the Step 3 synergy estimate to reach a go/no-go call, or a defensible price range.
5. **Alternative Solution** — if the analysis says don't do the deal (or the preferred target turns out to be
   unavailable), loop back to Step 2 and re-run Steps 2-4 against the next-best candidate.

**B. Short/interview-case format — a lighter five steps, judged on reasoning quality more than precision.**
1. **Identify the M&A reason** — ask directly (of the judge/interviewer, or of the brief's own stated facts) if
   it isn't given.
2. **Quantify the objective** — pin down the numeric target (an ROI, a payback window, a growth percentage) —
   again, ask if it isn't given.
3. **Solve via framework** — score the deal against four lenses: Market Attractiveness, Target & Buyer, Synergy
   (often absent for a pure financial-buyer deal), and Value Creation (the lens that actually drives any
   quantitative ROI/payback math).
4. **Consider risk and alternatives** — what could break the recommendation, and what's the next-best option if
   this deal doesn't happen.
5. **Recommend and lay out an action plan** — either a "proceed" or "don't proceed" call is acceptable here; what
   matters is that the reasoning is logical and the structure is clear, not which side of the call is taken.

Each stage in either format depends on the one before it — don't jump to a valuation before Strategic Fit has
actually been established, and don't force the full five-step long-case process onto a 30-minute interview
format that hands over limited data and expects you to ask for the rest.

Worked examples are not included in this public copy.

## 5. What information each step needs

If the checklist confirms an M&A case (in full or in part), here's what the framework asks for at each step.
This list is deliberately generic — it names the *kind* of data each step needs, not a specific team's files, so
it stays valid regardless of which company or competition it's applied to.

| Framework step | Data it needs |
|---|---|
| M&A Objective (Porter's Five Forces) | General knowledge of the acquirer's industry structure — competitive intensity, supplier/buyer power, entry barriers, substitute threats — built live and confirmed against the brief |
| Company Analysis (SWOT / Strategic Fit) | Whatever the brief states or implies about both companies' products, technology, target market, culture, and supply-chain position — case-specific, not pre-buildable |
| Financial Analysis (Synergy tree) | Historical revenue/cost data for both companies, plus category/industry benchmarks for realistic revenue-growth and cost-reduction assumptions |
| Acquisition Feasibility (DCF) | Projected free cash flow for the target (or a way to derive it from given revenue/margin data), a defensible discount rate for the target's industry, and a long-run growth-rate assumption |
| Alternative Solution | The same Company-Analysis and Financial-Analysis inputs, applied to the next-best candidate target |
| Short-case: Market Attractiveness / Target & Buyer / Value Creation | Whatever operating figures the case hands over incrementally (pricing, capacity, cost structure) — deliberately incomplete at first; the skill being tested is asking for what's missing |

**Before the clock starts on an actual case, check your own team's prep against this list** — for each row, do
you already have this data, or will it have to come live from the brief? This is a heavier-prep case type than
some others: valuation mechanics (DCF, the synergy tree) are reusable regardless of company, but a real M&A case
usually leans hard on being comfortable with the arithmetic under time pressure, so drilling the DCF formula and
the synergy-tree structure until they're second nature matters more here than for a mostly-qualitative case type.

If your team keeps a research index for the current competition (which company, which files, which framework
step each one covers), treat it as a separate document alongside this skill, not part of it — that keeps this
skill reusable for a future competition without editing, and keeps this skill's *classification logic*
independent of any one team's research, which may be incomplete or unverified in places.

## 6. The case-solving process, if it's an M&A case

A suggested run of show once the checklist confirms this case type and the clock starts. Rescale the timing to
the actual deadline, but keep the order — each step depends on the one before it. Timings assume the
**long-case** format; a short/interview-format case compresses this whole sequence into ~30 minutes and skips
straight to asking clarifying questions rather than building out each step in full.

| Time (of ~10 working hrs) | Step | What the team does |
|---|---|---|
| First 20 min | Confirm case type | Run the Section 2 checklist as a group. Agree explicitly: "this is an M&A case, targeting [X]." Assign roles (Strategic-Fit lead, Financial/Synergy lead, Valuation lead, Risk/QA lead). |
| 20-60 min | SCQ + M&A Objective | Write the SCQ. Run Porter's Five Forces on the acquirer's industry to ground *why* this deal matters strategically, before any numbers are built. |
| 60-150 min | Company Analysis | SWOT both companies against the brief's facts/exhibits. Land on an explicit, named Strategic Fit story — which specific overlaps or complements actually drive the deal's logic. |
| 150-270 min | Financial Analysis | Build the Synergy tree. Ground revenue-growth and cost-reduction assumptions in the companies' own historical data plus stated industry benchmarks, rather than inventing numbers. |
| 270-360 min | Acquisition Feasibility | Value the target via DCF (or the case's preferred method). Compare against the Synergy estimate. Land on a go/no-go call and a defensible price range. |
| 360-420 min | Alternative Solution / Risk | If the call is "don't do this deal" (or the target may be unavailable), evaluate the next-best candidate. Either way, stress-test the recommendation against a changed assumption (what if growth or the discount rate moves). |
| 420-480 min | Recommendation | Draft the final recommendation, explicitly tied to the Step 1 Objective — not just "the numbers work," but "this is why the deal serves the strategic need." |
| Remaining time | Synthesis & rehearsal | Compress onto one cheat-sheet page: the Objective, the Strategic Fit summary, the headline valuation/synergy numbers, the recommendation, the top 1-2 risks. Rehearse the arc: Objective → Fit → Synergy → Valuation → recommendation. Time-box Q&A prep — expect challenges on the discount-rate/growth-rate assumptions and on how confident the Strategic Fit story really is. |

## 7. Common failure modes to avoid

- **Jumping straight to a valuation number without establishing Strategic Fit first.** A DCF output with no
  strategic story behind it reads as an arithmetic exercise, not a case answer — the numbers need to serve a
  reason the deal makes sense, not stand alone.
- **Inventing synergy assumptions with no grounding.** Revenue-growth and cost-reduction assumptions inside the
  synergy tree need to trace back to the brief's own data or a stated industry benchmark — an unsupported "10%
  more revenue" reads as a guess, not an analysis.
- **Treating the discount rate and growth-rate assumptions as afterthoughts.** These two numbers drive the whole
  valuation output; a case that glosses over where they came from invites the sharpest Q&A pushback.
- **Running the long-case five-step process onto a short/interview-format case.** A 30-minute format rewards
  asking for missing data and reasoning clearly under uncertainty, not a full five-step build — trying to force
  the whole long-case sequence into that window wastes the clock and misses what's actually being tested.
- **Confusing a strategic acquirer's deal with a financial buyer's deal.** A strategic acquirer cares about
  synergy and long-term fit; a financial buyer (e.g. a PE fund) mostly cares about standalone ROI/payback on
  the target itself — using the wrong lens (chasing synergy in a pure financial-buyer case, or ignoring ROI/
  payback math in a strategic-acquirer case) misreads what the case is actually testing.
- **Picking a side and refusing to show the other.** Since either a "proceed" or "don't proceed" call is
  typically acceptable, a recommendation that doesn't also name the strongest case for the *other* side (even
  briefly) reads as less rigorous than one that does.

## 8. One-page decision flow (first few minutes)

1. Read the brief once, cover to cover, before discussing — don't let the first person's guess anchor the room.
2. Underline every mention of a second company, a deal, a price, an ROI/payback target, or "should we
   acquire/merge/partner/invest" vs. a perception/behaviour change, a pure internal cost-benefit ask on the
   existing business, or a general growth-direction question.
3. Score the case against the Section 2 checklist as a group — out loud, fast, don't overthink individual boxes.
4. If it's an M&A case: decide long-case or short/interview format, open your team's prepped industry/valuation
   knowledge, assign roles, start the Section 6 timeline (or, for the short format, go straight to asking
   clarifying questions).
5. If it's mixed: agree which sub-question gets this treatment and which gets a different framework (see
   Section 3), and say so explicitly in the final answer.
6. If it's clearly something else entirely: set this skill aside and use the matching case-type skill instead.
