# Market Entry Case — Checklist and Framework

This skill turns a case-solving method into something usable live, during a competition, under
time pressure. It is one member of a family of case-type skills (`case-brand-communication`,
`case-profit-optimization`, `case-supply-chain`, `case-ma`, `case-product-launch`, `case-investment`,
`case-market-entry`, and later `case-growth-strategy`, `case-turnaround`, `case-pricing`) that together cover the
case types a team might draw. Read the "How this fits with other case-type skills" section below first if more
than one case-type skill is loaded — it explains how to keep the final answer coherent instead of stapling
together separate mini-answers.

The content here is an original, rewritten summary of standard market-entry and market-sizing methods (3Cs
analysis, MECE market segmentation, demand-side/supply-side market sizing, entry-mode comparison, payback/ROI
gut-checks). Keep this skill team-internal; don't redistribute outside the competition team.

**By design, this skill is company-agnostic.** It knows how to recognise a market-entry case and how to solve
one — it does not know anything about any specific company, real or fictional, and doesn't reference any
particular team's research files by name. That's deliberate: the checklist and framework need to work unchanged
on whatever brief actually drops, for this competition or a future one, without being tuned (or biased) toward
one team's prep material. If your team has built company-specific research for the current competition, keep the
pointer to it in a separate, clearly-labelled file outside this skill (see Section 5) — don't fold it into this
skill's own content.

**Format note.** This skill's source material is built around the shorter "case interview" format (a compressed
15-30 minute individual exercise) rather than the full multi-hour "business case" competition format the other
skills in this family are built around. The framework itself doesn't change for a 48-hour competition — market
entry is market entry either way — but treat the process below as **scalable**: compressed to a quick read-and-
triage when time is short, expanded with deeper research and richer data when the full competition clock is
available, the same way `case-ma` scales its process across a long-case vs. short/interview-case split.

## 1. Where "Market Entry" sits among case types

A 48-hour business case can land in several different problem families. A Market Entry case is specifically
about *whether to enter a new market, segment, geography, or channel, and how* — not about a deal with a
specific already-named company, fixing today's existing business, or launching a new product in a market
that's already a given.

| Case type | Core question | Primary tool |
|---|---|---|
| **Market Entry** | Should we enter this new market/segment/format, and how? | 3Cs + Market + Financials → Market Sizing → entry-mode fit → go/no-go |
| Brand Communication | How do we change perception/behaviour toward a brand through a campaign or message? | Issue → Objective → SoG → Winning Point → J2BD → Insight → Idea → IMC → Trade → KPI |
| Profit Optimization | Why is profit falling (or would a proposed move be profitable), and what fixes it? | Profit tree (Revenue − Cost, MECE by industry) → branch analysis → root cause → recommendation |
| Supply Chain / Operations | Why can't the chain deliver goods/materials reliably or efficiently, and how do we fix it? | Demand/Inventory/Supply diagnostic → qualitative (disruption) or quantitative (optimization) solving path |
| M&A / Partnership | Should we buy, merge with, invest in, or partner with another company, and on what terms? | Porter's Five Forces (objective) → SWOT/Strategic Fit → Synergy tree → DCF valuation → go/no-go |
| New Product Launch | Should we launch this new product/feature, what should it look like, and how do we bring it to market? | STP → Whitespace × Strength positioning → Proposition/Positioning → 6Ps go-to-market → revenue/P&L goal |
| Finance / Investment | Is this business financially healthy, and is a specific investment worth the capital it needs? | P&L read → Payback Period / Investment Value / ROIC |
| Growth Strategy | Where should the next unit of growth come from? | Ansoff matrix, portfolio/SoG-style analysis |
| Turnaround / Crisis | How do we stop the bleeding and stabilise after a shock? | Root-cause diagnosis, stakeholder triage, phased plan |
| Pricing | Is the price right, and how should it change? | Elasticity, competitor benchmarking, value-based pricing |

A live brief is rarely a pure type. "Should we enter this new market" could be Market Entry (the deliverable is
a qualitative go/no-go on the market itself, weighing attractiveness, fit, and entry mode) or `case-investment`
(the deliverable is a payback/ROIC number on the capital the entry requires, with the market choice already
narrowed or settled). "Should we enter with a brand-new product" mixes Market Entry with New Product Launch —
the market question and the product-concept question are genuinely two different sub-questions. The checklist
below is how the team tells which one it's actually holding.

## 2. Checklist — is this a Market Entry case?

Run this in the first 15-20 minutes after the brief drops, out loud, as a group. Score it loosely — the more
left-column boxes get ticked, the more confidently the team can commit to this framework.

**Signals it IS a Market Entry case**
- The brief asks whether a company should enter a new geography, segment, channel, or format — framed as a
  strategic-fit and attractiveness question, not purely "would it be profitable" (which is profit optimization
  wearing an entry costume) or purely "what's the payback/ROI number" (which is `case-investment`).
- The deliverable sounds like "should we enter, and if so how" — a go/no-go plus a recommended entry approach,
  not a deal-specific valuation, a campaign, or a product concept.
- The brief needs (or explicitly asks for) a market-size estimate for the new market/segment.
- An entry-mode choice is in play — organic/greenfield build, joint venture, acquisition, or franchise — as a
  genuinely open question, not one already settled by the brief.
- The brief gives (or asks the team to reason about) competitor landscape, customer fit, and company-readiness
  information specific to the *new* market, not just the company's existing home market.

**Signals it's something else (redirect to the matching skill)**
- The ask is "is our current business profitable / what's dragging profit down," with no new market in view →
  Profit Optimization.
- The real test is the investment math — payback period, ROIC, a multi-year financial projection — on an entry
  investment where the market choice is already narrowed or given, rather than a qualitative judgment on which
  market and how → `case-investment`.
- A specific acquisition target is already named, and the ask centers on that target's strategic fit and a
  go/no-go/price call on that one deal → `case-ma`; Market Entry may weigh acquisition as one *option* among
  several entry modes without going deep on one target's own valuation.
- The ask centers on building a new product concept — positioning, target segment, go-to-market plan — with the
  market itself already a given, not an open question → New Product Launch.

**When it's mixed:** many real prompts blend types — e.g. a market-entry case that narrows down to "and
specifically, what product should we launch to enter this market" (hand the product-concept sub-question to
`case-product-launch`), or one that narrows down to "and specifically, is the required investment worth it on
the numbers" (hand that sub-question to `case-investment`). Scope this framework to the specific sub-question
that's actually about which market and how to enter it, and hand the rest to whichever skill matches the
broader strategic question.

## 3. How this fits with other case-type skills

**If `case-orchestrator` is loaded, it now owns this logic centrally.** It reads the whole brief, segments it
into sub-questions, classifies each one against every case-type skill's checklist, and integrates every
sub-framework's output into one coherent case structure tied to a shared objective. Load `case-orchestrator`
first and let it hand off to this skill for any confirmed Market Entry sub-question, rather than re-deriving the
classification here. The rest of this section is this skill's own standalone fallback logic, for when
`case-orchestrator` isn't loaded and this skill is being used on its own.

The plan is for this skill to sit alongside siblings for other case types (brand communication, profit
optimization, supply chain, M&A, product launch, investment, growth strategy, turnaround, pricing, etc.), each
with its own checklist and framework. When more than one is available:

1. **Run every available checklist against the brief before committing to one.** Real briefs blend types more
   often than they're pure.
2. **If exactly one lens fires strongly**, use that skill's framework as the backbone of the whole answer.
3. **If more than one lens fires (a mixed case)**, don't let each framework run in isolation and get stapled
   together at the end. Nominate one owner for the shared throughline — one SCQ (Situation-Complication-Question)
   and one overarching Objective that every sub-framework's output has to tie back to. Each specialized section
   should open by naming which sub-question it's answering and close by connecting back to that shared
   Objective, so the final answer reads as one solution arrived at from multiple angles, not several
   disconnected mini-decks.
4. **If nothing fires clearly**, don't force this framework. Say so, and fall back to general business-case
   structuring (root cause → objective → options → recommendation) rather than distorting the case to fit a
   template that doesn't match.

**Specifically with `case-product-launch`:** if the entry vehicle is an all-new product (not an existing product
entering a new geography), the case mixes Market Entry with Product Launch — this skill owns "which market, is
it worth entering," `case-product-launch` owns "what should the new product itself be" once the market is a
given. If the market choice is still genuinely open, this skill leads with `case-product-launch`'s Product
Development steps feeding in as supporting detail; if the market is already settled and the brief is really
asking what to build and how to launch it there, `case-product-launch` owns the sub-question outright. This
mirrors the overlap note already written into `case-product-launch/SKILL.md` Section 3.

**Specifically with `case-investment`:** this skill's Financials pillar (Step 2 below) uses a light version of
`case-investment`'s Payback Period / ROI formulas as a quick gut-check on the entry investment. The test for
which skill owns the sub-question: if the real test is a qualitative judgment on market attractiveness, customer
fit, and entry-mode choice, that's this skill; if the real test is the investment-appraisal math itself (multi-
year projections, discount-rate sensitivity, comparing entry modes purely on the numbers), hand off to
`case-investment` in full.

**Specifically with `case-ma`:** when the entry mode under consideration is specifically acquiring a local
player, this skill weighs acquisition as one *option* on the entry-mode menu (Step 2's Company pillar); once a
specific target is named and the test becomes that target's own strategic fit and valuation, hand off to
`case-ma`.

As sibling skills get added, keep them in the same `case-<type>` naming pattern so they're easy to spot as one
family.

## 4. The framework, step by step

Market Entry draws on two related tools: **Market Sizing** (a sub-tool used heavily inside Step 3, but also a
case type in its own right when a brief asks purely "how big is this market" with no entry decision attached)
and the **4-step Market Entry process** itself. The roadmap below is a summary. This public copy does not include
the full worked detail — the demand-side/supply-side issue-tree method, the 3Cs+Market+Financials framework
broken into sub-questions, or the case-math formula families — or worked examples (kept out deliberately, see the
repo README) — read it as a checklist and step outline, not a complete how-to.

**A. Market Sizing — four steps.**
1. **Clarifying** — pin down whether the answer is wanted in value or volume, over what timeframe, for what
   geography, and for what exact product scope; then spend a minute building the issue tree before answering.
2. **Segment** — split the population into MECE segments (demographic, geographic, psychographic, or
   behavioral, whichever best fits the product).
3. **Assumptions & Calculations** — for each branch, propose a grounded assumption, state the reasoning, and
   multiply through the tree; round for speed without compounding errors.
4. **Insight** — sense-check the result against a real-world reference point, revise the assumption that's most
   likely off if the number looks implausible, then draw a conclusion relevant to the actual question.

**B. Market Entry — four steps, each building on the one before it.**
1. **Understand the Case Question (SCQ)** — pin down exactly what's being asked; flag immediately if the brief
   is really a Market Entry × Product Launch mix (an all-new product is the entry vehicle).
2. **Plan your Structure** — default framework is 3Cs + Market, plus Financials: Market & Competitor (size,
   trend, competitor landscape), Customer (target customer, need/proposition fit), Company (production
   readiness, scalability, competitive advantage), Financials (capital required, expected payback/ROI).
3. **Information Analysis & Calculation** — size the addressable market (Part A), apply a target capture rate to
   estimate the company's own achievable revenue, then run the light Financial/Investment-Analysis formulas
   (Break-even, ROI) to reach a payback figure.
4. **Assert a Conclusion** — lead with the direct answer (enter / don't enter), then supporting reasons, then
   explicit risks (funding availability, cannibalization of existing lines), then a recommendation scaled to the
   company's actual resources (e.g. a small-scale pilot if capital is constrained, rather than a full launch
   either way).

Worked examples are not included in this public copy.

## 5. What information each step needs

If the checklist confirms a Market Entry case (in full or in part), here's what the framework asks for at each
step. This list is deliberately generic — it names the *kind* of data each step needs, not a specific team's
files, so it stays valid regardless of which company or competition it's applied to.

| Framework step | Data it needs |
|---|---|
| Market & Competitor | Market size (or enough data to size it), market growth trend, competitor landscape (key players, share, positioning) in the *new* market |
| Customer | Target customer profile in the new market, and whether the company's existing product benefit/proposition actually fits that customer's needs |
| Company | The company's production/process readiness (existing capacity vs. new investment needed), ability to scale, and any transferable competitive advantage |
| Financials | Capital required to enter (R&D, technology, customer-acquisition, or build-out cost), plus whatever revenue/cost data is needed for a payback/ROI gut-check |

**Before the clock starts on an actual case, check your own team's prep against this list** — for each row, do
you already have this data, or will it have to come live from the brief? This is a comparatively qualitative
case type layered on top of a genuinely quantitative sub-skill (Market Sizing): the 3Cs+Market+Financials
structure is the reusable muscle memory worth drilling, while the Market Sizing issue-tree method needs enough
live practice that segmenting and estimating under time pressure feels natural rather than improvised.

If your team keeps a research index for the current competition (which company, which files, which framework
step each one covers), treat it as a separate document alongside this skill, not part of it — that keeps this
skill reusable for a future competition without editing, and keeps this skill's *classification logic*
independent of any one team's research, which may be incomplete or unverified in places.

## 6. The case-solving process, if it's a Market Entry case

A suggested run of show once the checklist confirms this case type and the clock starts. Rescale the timing to
the actual deadline, but keep the order — each step depends on the one before it. This skill's own source
material is built for a compressed 15-30 minute interview format; the timings below scale that same 4-step
process up to a full competition clock rather than changing the steps themselves.

| Time (of ~10 working hrs) | Step | What the team does |
|---|---|---|
| First 20 min | Confirm case type | Run the Section 2 checklist as a group. Agree explicitly: "this is a Market Entry case, into [market/segment]." Flag immediately if it's mixed with Product Launch. Assign roles (Market/Competitor lead, Customer lead, Company/Financials lead, Risk/QA lead). |
| 20-60 min | SCQ + Structure | Write the SCQ. Lay out the 3Cs+Market+Financials framework and confirm which pillars the brief actually gives data for vs. which need live estimation. |
| 60-180 min | Market & Competitor + Customer | Size the market (Part A's 4-step Market Sizing process if not given directly). Research/reason through the competitor landscape and confirm customer-need fit. |
| 180-270 min | Company + Financials | Assess production/scale readiness and any transferable advantage. Apply a target capture rate to the sized market, then run the payback/ROI gut-check. |
| 270-330 min | Recommendation | Draft the enter/don't-enter call, explicitly tied back to the Step 1 SCQ, with the entry-mode recommendation if one is warranted. |
| 330-390 min | Risk / stress-test | Name the top 1-2 risks explicitly (funding, cannibalization, competitive response) and, if capital is constrained, propose a scaled-down pilot alternative. |
| Remaining time | Synthesis & rehearsal | Compress onto one cheat-sheet page: the market size, the customer/competitor read, the entry-mode recommendation, the payback figure, the top 1-2 risks. Rehearse the arc: Market Attractiveness → Customer/Company Fit → Financials → Recommendation. Time-box Q&A prep — expect challenges on the market-sizing assumptions and the capture-rate estimate. |

## 7. Common failure modes to avoid

- **Sizing the market without stating assumptions out loud.** A market-size figure presented with no visible
  reasoning reads as looked-up rather than derived — always show the issue tree and name each assumption as
  it's made.
- **Skipping the sense-check on the final sized number.** An implausible per-person or per-customer spend figure
  that goes unchallenged undermines the whole answer's credibility — always divide back down to a per-person
  reference point and revise the assumption that's most likely off if it doesn't feel realistic.
- **Treating a market-entry case as automatically pure Market Entry.** If the brief's entry vehicle is an
  all-new product, failing to flag the Product Launch overlap in Step 1 means the product-concept work either
  gets skipped or gets force-fit into this skill's framework, which isn't built for it.
- **Leading a conclusion with the numbers before naming the risks.** Section 4B's Step 4 explicitly wants the
  concerns (funding, cannibalization) named before the final recommendation — a conclusion that only reports
  "the numbers work" without naming what could go wrong reads as less rigorous.
- **Comparing entry modes only on the qualitative pros/cons table without ever running the numbers, or vice
  versa.** Both halves matter — a stated preference for one entry mode needs the payback/ROI gut-check to back
  it up, and a numbers-only recommendation needs the qualitative trade-offs (control, speed, local experience)
  named alongside it, the same failure mode `case-investment` flags for its own entry-mode comparison.
- **Running the full multi-year Investment Appraisal math when the case only needs a quick payback/ROI
  gut-check.** If the investment math itself isn't the actual test, use this skill's own light formulas rather
  than importing `case-investment`'s full depth and eating time that should go to the market and customer
  analysis.

## 8. One-page decision flow (first few minutes)

1. Read the brief once, cover to cover, before discussing — don't let the first person's guess anchor the room.
2. Underline every mention of a new geography/segment/channel, "should we enter," an entry-mode choice, or a
   market-size ask, vs. a root-cause profit-decline ask on the existing business, a named acquisition target, or
   a new-product-concept ask where the market is already a given.
3. Score the case against the Section 2 checklist as a group — out loud, fast, don't overthink individual boxes.
4. If it's a Market Entry case: check whether it's mixed with Product Launch or Investment (Section 3), open
   your team's prepped 3Cs/Market-Sizing muscle memory, assign roles, start the Section 6 timeline.
5. If it's mixed: agree which sub-question gets this treatment and which gets a different framework (see
   Section 3), and say so explicitly in the final answer.
6. If it's clearly something else entirely: set this skill aside and use the matching case-type skill instead.
